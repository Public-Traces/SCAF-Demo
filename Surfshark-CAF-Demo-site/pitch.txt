The cybersecurity need:

The internet's content and traffic are now AI-led. In 2024, automated traffic overtook human traffic for the first time, reaching about 51% of all web traffic, with malicious bots making up roughly a third. For decades, we have operated on an assumption that sophisticated behavior on the internet has, by default, a person's intent behind it. As AI lowered the cost and accessibility of generating content, the unit economics have shifted the internet towards a cesspool of unauthored — and increasingly unauthorized — creation and consumption. Attackers now exploit this assumption at scale: phishing, social engineering, sock-puppet influence operations, fake reviews, and account fraud all depend on synthetic activity passing as human.

The internet cannot reach its full potential if the human effort that underpins it becomes indistinguishable from automation. Today's leading response, AI watermarking, asks AI to label itself, a safeguard bad actors will simply skip. We bet the future of web security lies in transparency in the other direction: assuring humanity, not detecting artifice. Security teams have long recognized that allowlisting what is trusted outperforms blocklisting every threat. Proof of humanity applies the same logic, placing the burden on the honest party to prove rather than on defenders to guess.

Our idea:

We propose a novel cryptographic verification tool for humans to demonstrate that their own words were physically typed. Our tool adds a certificate of human origin that travels with text written on desktops. With the privacy-preserving tool downloaded on participating desktops, proofs are generated passively, with no extra effort from the writer, and verification works seamlessly across writing environments. Our tool can (1) certify that text was physically typed by a human, (2) verify that certification wherever the text appears online, and (3) attribute it to its author. Attribution stays in the author's control: they can choose to link the text to a verified identity or remain an anonymous-but-verified human. Because a tool that observes typing must itself be secure, typing signals are processed on-device, raw keystrokes never leave the machine, and attestations are cryptographically signed. The system is designed to resist spoofing by software-injected keystrokes and hardware typing emulators.

Unlike AI detection tools, we let humans constructively prove their human involvement with digital actions and enable that proof to travel alongside their actions across the digital world. Our proposal stands to make the human web more transparent, more distinct, and more resilient.

In doing so, we also seek to raise the cost of passing off artificial content as human. In our vision, everyone on the open web benefits: writers seeking recognition, teachers seeking assurance of student authorship to determine capability, platforms fighting coordinated bot campaigns, security teams defending against AI-generated phishing, and hiring managers screening a flood of synthetic applications.

How users will experience it: 

Writers download a desktop app and deploy it in the background of their normal computer usage. Signatures travel with the text in their native format, across the open Web. Users can optionally attach a verification link. Readers of content, keen to understand if text was indeed typed or artificially generated, can go to a verifier site to check the text. Think of it as the inverse of Claude’s new detection tool.
